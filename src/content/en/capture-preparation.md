## First: where is the photograph worth taking?

A remarkable view is the starting point. A gigapixel location also needs to work photographically and practically: which landscape should the image show, which details will reward zooming, and do trees, railings or other obstacles block part of the intended coverage? A wonderful viewpoint may offer little room for equipment.

### How do I get there with more than ten kilograms of equipment?

Camera, lens, panorama head, tripod, batteries and accessories can total **10 kg or more**. Consider access when choosing the location: walking distance, elevation gain, steps or ladders, and whether the equipment can actually be carried along that route. A short distance on a map may be demanding with bulky luggage.

Plan the journey, final walk and any transport such as a cable car. Its current operating times and the return journey must fit the capture window. Allow for walking and setup with the load, as well as getting back after a long sequence. A lighter setup or another viewpoint may be more practical than an impressive location that is difficult to reach with the necessary equipment.

### Weather, distant visibility and light: is the window suitable?

| Condition | What to check when planning and on location |
| --- | --- |
| Wind and gusts | Can the equipment remain still, particularly with a long lens at an exposed viewpoint? |
| Clouds | Will illumination stay similar, or will cloud shadows move rapidly across the scene? |
| Clear air and distant visibility | Are the intended distant subjects actually distinguishable? An unobstructed view does not guarantee fine detail. |
| Haze | How much contrast is lost in distant scenery? Later dehazing has limits. |
| Atmospheric turbulence | Do distant details shimmer or distort in magnified live view or test photographs? Clear air is not necessarily steady air. |
| Changing light | Will brightness and shadows remain reasonably consistent throughout the sequence, or change substantially within minutes? |

Conditions need to suit **the whole sequence**, rather than just the first attractive test photograph. Cloud shadows and rapidly changing light complicate consistency; wind and atmospheric turbulence can limit captured detail. [Nikon identifies heat haze as a possible cause of blurred telephoto images.](https://onlinemanual.nikonimglib.com/faq/en/shooting_issues/images_not_sharp.html)

### Visitors and space: share the viewpoint

On a small viewing platform I do not want the tripod, head and bags to occupy all the space. Before setting up, check that equipment, camera movement and passing visitors all have room. Keep routes and access to the view clear, and store bags and accessories compactly.

Visitors may also walk through the frame or disturb the setup. A quieter time, smaller setup, different position or alternative viewpoint may work better. PTGui masks can correct some moving objects later; they do not replace a suitable location or consideration for other visitors.

## Choose the capture type and lens

A **landscape gigapixel panorama** shows a selected landscape section in very high detail. Long focal lengths of **100–400 mm** are useful because each photograph records a small part of the scene. A **high-resolution spherical panorama** captures the surrounding space, including the zenith and nadir for a complete sphere. For this I tend to use focal lengths of **28–100 mm**. It can also reach gigapixel resolution: coverage and pixel count describe different properties.

The lens follows the intended result. With the same camera, a longer focal length gives a narrower field of view and a larger image of distant details, but requires more photographs and time for the same overall coverage. Spherical capture also needs attention to nearby objects, depth of field and clearance as the camera tilts. A fisheye can reduce image count, but is not required for a spherical panorama. Edge sharpness and vignetting at the intended aperture matter too. Keep a zoom lens at the same focal length throughout the sequence.

## Camera check

### Hardware check

- **Clean lens:** inspect the lens's optical surfaces for dirt.
- **Clean sensor:** check a test photograph for noticeable spots caused by dust particles on the sensor.
- **Charged batteries:** check the camera, panorama head and control device, and pack charged spare batteries.

### Dust particles on the sensor

Dust particles on the sensor appear as spots, particularly in a smooth sky, and recur across the sequence. I change lenses infrequently to limit dust ingress. Before a large panorama I check a test photograph for noticeable dust particles on the sensor.

In my experience, cleaning with a **sensor swab** should only rarely be necessary: once several noticeable dust particles have accumulated on the sensor.

I have not been satisfied with the software corrections I tried, and a blower alone did not reliably remove stubborn dust. Suitable **sensor-cleaning swabs** worked very well for me once I finally tried them. Before wiping, I use a blower to dislodge loose, particularly hard particles so they are not dragged across the surface and cause scratches.

This describes my experience, rather than a general cleaning procedure. Swabs, fluid and method must suit the camera. Anyone unsure of the risks or the model-specific instructions should use a service centre. [Sony describes in-camera cleaning and a suitable blower first, and recommends service if dust remains.](https://support.d-imaging.sony.co.jp/www/support/ilc/sensor/en.html)
